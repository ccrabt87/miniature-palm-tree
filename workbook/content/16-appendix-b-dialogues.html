Appendix B

Eight Bedside Dialogues

Phrase tables teach you words. Dialogues teach you where the words go — and, more usefully, where a real conversation stops being yours and becomes the interpreter's. Read each one out loud, taking the nurse's part.

How to practise these

  1. Read the whole dialogue once, silently, for the shape of it.
  2. Read it again out loud, both parts, at normal speaking speed.
  3. Cover the English column and read only the Spanish.
  4. Cover the Spanish and try to produce the nurse's lines from the English.
  5. Read the note at the end — the note is the actual lesson.

Dialogue 1 — First five minutes of an admission

Sra. Ochoa, 68, admitted from the ED with abdominal pain. Her son is at the bedside. You have not yet called the interpreter.

Nurse

Buenas tardes. Me llamo Dani y soy su enfermera esta tarde.

Good afternoon. My name is Dani and I’m your nurse this afternoon.

Nurse

¿Cómo se llama usted?

What is your name?

Patient

María Ochoa Salazar.

María Ochoa Salazar.

Nurse

¿Y su fecha de nacimiento?

And your date of birth?

Patient

El cuatro de marzo de mil novecientos cincuenta y siete.

March fourth, 1957.

Nurse

Gracias. ¿Cómo prefiere que le llame?

Thank you. How would you like me to address you?

Patient

Doña María está bien.

Doña María is fine.

Nurse

Doña María, tenemos intérpretes gratis. ¿Quiere que llame a uno?

Doña María, we have free interpreters. Would you like me to call one?

Family

No hace falta, yo le traduzco.

That’s not necessary, I’ll translate for her.

Nurse

Gracias — se ve cuánto ayuda a su mamá. Pero el hospital me exige usar un intérprete profesional para la entrevista.

Thank you — I can see how much you help your mother. But the hospital requires me to use a professional interpreter for the interview.

Nurse

Es gratis y tarda dos minutos. Por favor quédese — lo quiero aquí como su familia, no como intérprete.

It’s free and takes two minutes. Please stay — I want you here as her family, not as the interpreter.

[ You dial the interpreter line from the room phone and stay at the bedside while it connects. ]

Nurse

Ya viene el intérprete. Mientras tanto, ¿tiene dolor ahora?

The interpreter is coming. In the meantime, are you in pain right now?

Patient

Sí, aquí.

Yes, here.

Nurse

¿Del cero al diez, qué número?

Zero to ten, what number?

Patient

Como seis.

About a six.

Nurse

Gracias. En cuanto entre el intérprete, le pregunto todo lo demás.

Thank you. As soon as the interpreter is on, I’ll ask you everything else.

What made this work

Everything before the interpreter connects is orientation and a pain number — safe territory. The history itself waits. Note that the refusal to use the son is framed as a rule about the hospital and a role for him, never as doubt about his Spanish.

Dialogue 2 — A pain check at two in the morning

Post-op day one. You are doing rounds and the patient is awake, guarding, and quiet.

Nurse

Perdone que lo despierte, don Roberto. ¿Cómo va el dolor?

Sorry to wake you, don Roberto. How is the pain?

Patient

Ahí va. No es nada.

It’s all right. It’s nothing.

Nurse

¿Del cero al diez?

Zero to ten?

Patient

Tres, cuatro.

Three, four.

[ He is diaphoretic and has not moved position in two hours. ]

Nurse

¿Pudo dormir con este dolor?

Have you been able to sleep with this pain?

Patient

No mucho.

Not much.

Nurse

¿Puede voltearse de lado sin que le duela más?

Can you turn on your side without it hurting more?

Patient

No. Cuando me muevo, se pone feo.

No. When I move, it gets bad.

Nurse

Entonces vamos a tratarlo mejor. Le puedo traer algo para el dolor ahora.

Then let’s treat it better. I can bring you something for the pain now.

Patient

No quiero molestar.

I don’t want to be a bother.

Nurse

No molesta. Es mi trabajo, y el dolor le impide sanar.

You’re not a bother. It’s my job, and pain keeps you from healing.

Nurse

Va a tardar unos veinte minutos en hacer efecto. Regreso a ver cómo sigue.

It will take about twenty minutes to work. I’ll come back to check on you.

What made this work

The number was three. The function questions — can you sleep, can you turn — told the truth. «No quiero molestar» is one of the most common sentences in a Spanish-speaking patient’s night, and it deserves a direct answer.

Dialogue 3 — A refused medication

Morning med pass. The patient waves the cup away.

Nurse

Le traigo su medicina de la mañana.

I’m bringing your morning medication.

Patient

No, gracias. No la quiero.

No, thank you. I don’t want it.

Nurse

Está bien. ¿Me puede decir por qué?

That’s fine. Can you tell me why?

Patient

Ya me la tomé en la casa antes de venir.

I already took it at home before coming in.

Nurse

¿A qué hora la tomó?

What time did you take it?

Patient

Como a las seis.

Around six.

Nurse

Gracias por decirme. Eso es muy importante.

Thank you for telling me. That is very important.

Nurse

No se la voy a dar ahora. Voy a avisarle al doctor para no repetir la dosis.

I’m not going to give it now. I’ll let the doctor know so we don’t repeat the dose.

Nurse

¿Trajo sus medicinas de casa o una lista?

Did you bring your medications from home, or a list?

Patient

Mi hija las tiene en una bolsa.

My daughter has them in a bag.

Nurse

Perfecto. ¿Le puede pedir que las traiga hoy?

Perfect. Can you ask her to bring them today?

What made this work

A refusal answered with «why» found a duplicate dose. Charted as «patient refused» and nothing else, this becomes a medication error two hours later.

Dialogue 4 — Pre-op, and the family’s questions

Surgery at 07:30. The patient’s daughter arrives at 06:00 and finds her mother with nothing on the breakfast tray.

Nurse

Buenos días. Hoy es la cirugía a las siete y media.

Good morning. Surgery is today at seven thirty.

Family

¿Por qué no le han dado de comer?

Why haven’t they fed her?

Nurse

Porque no puede comer ni beber nada antes de la anestesia. Es por seguridad.

Because she can’t eat or drink anything before anesthesia. It’s for safety.

Family

¿Ni agua?

Not even water?

Nurse

Ni agua. Después de la cirugía le vamos a dar líquidos poco a poco.

Not even water. After surgery we’ll give her fluids little by little.

Patient

Tengo mucha sed.

I’m very thirsty.

Nurse

Lo sé, y lo siento. Le puedo humedecer los labios con una gasita.

I know, and I’m sorry. I can moisten your lips with a small piece of gauze.

Family

¿Cuánto va a durar?

How long will it take?

Nurse

El doctor le va a explicar todo eso. Voy a llamar al intérprete para que hablen bien.

The doctor will explain all of that. I’ll call the interpreter so you can talk properly.

Nurse

Mientras tanto: alguien va a venir por ella como a las siete. Usted puede acompañarnos hasta la puerta.

In the meantime: someone will come for her around seven. You can come with us as far as the door.

Family

¿Y dónde espero?

And where do I wait?

Nurse

En la sala de espera del segundo piso. Ahí le avisan cuando termine.

In the second-floor waiting room. They’ll let you know there when it’s over.

What made this work

Duration, risks, and what the surgery involves all belong to the surgeon and the interpreter. NPO status, logistics, and comfort measures are yours — and answering those well is what keeps the family from filling the silence with fear.

Dialogue 5 — A family update in the hallway

The patient has been taken for an urgent CT. Three family members are standing outside the room.

Nurse

Buenas noches. Soy la enfermera de su papá.

Good evening. I’m your father’s nurse.

Family

¿Qué pasó? ¿Está bien?

What happened? Is he all right?

Nurse

Se lo llevaron a un estudio del cerebro. Está estable y no está solo.

They took him for a scan of his brain. He is stable and he is not alone.

Family

¿Es grave?

Is it serious?

Nurse

El doctor le va a explicar los resultados en cuanto los tenga. Ya pedí un intérprete para esa conversación.

The doctor will explain the results as soon as he has them. I’ve already requested an interpreter for that conversation.

Nurse

¿Quién de ustedes va a ser el contacto? Así les doy las noticias a una persona y no se pierde nada.

Which of you will be the contact? That way I give updates to one person and nothing gets lost.

Family

Yo, soy la hija mayor.

Me, I’m the oldest daughter.

Nurse

Perfecto. ¿Me da su número?

Perfect. Can you give me your number?

Nurse

Van a tardar como cuarenta minutos. La sala de espera está aquí a la derecha.

It will take about forty minutes. The waiting room is here on the right.

Nurse

En cuanto regrese, vengo a avisarles.

As soon as he’s back, I’ll come tell you.

What made this work

Three things got done: a factual status without a prognosis, an interpreter already requested for the conversation that matters, and one named spokesperson. «¿Es grave?» is not yours to answer.

Dialogue 6 — Placing the interpreter call with the patient watching

You are about to teach a new insulin regimen. The video cart is at the bedside.

Nurse

Doña Elena, esto es importante y quiero explicárselo bien.

Doña Elena, this is important and I want to explain it to you properly.

Nurse

Voy a llamar a un intérprete por video. Es gratis y no aparece en su cuenta.

I’m going to call a video interpreter. It’s free and it doesn’t appear on your bill.

Patient

No quiero molestar a nadie.

I don’t want to trouble anyone.

Nurse

No molesta a nadie — es su trabajo, y están las veinticuatro horas.

You’re not troubling anyone — it’s their job, and they’re there twenty-four hours.

[ Turn the screen toward the patient, not toward yourself. ]

Interp.

Buenas tardes, soy el intérprete número 4471. Todo lo que se diga es confidencial.

Good afternoon, I’m interpreter number 4471. Everything said is confidential.

Nurse

Doña Elena, ¿escucha bien al intérprete?

Doña Elena, can you hear the interpreter well?

Patient

Sí, muy bien.

Yes, very well.

Nurse

¿Está bien este intérprete para usted, o prefiere una mujer?

Is this interpreter all right for you, or would you prefer a woman?

Patient

Así está bien.

This is fine.

[ From here, speak to the patient in English in short sentences, look at her while the interpreter speaks, and finish with teach-back. ]

What made this work

Four seconds of setup — free, confidential, can you hear, is this person all right — prevents most mid-encounter interpreter refusals.

Dialogue 7 — Reinforcing discharge teaching after the interpreter has gone

The interpreted discharge conversation is finished. You have ten minutes with the patient while transport is arranged.

Nurse

Antes de irse, dígame con sus palabras qué va a hacer en casa.

Before you go, tell me in your own words what you’re going to do at home.

Patient

Tomar la pastilla blanca en la mañana y la otra en la noche.

Take the white pill in the morning and the other one at night.

Nurse

Muy bien. ¿Y el antibiótico?

Very good. And the antibiotic?

Patient

Ese... ¿hasta que me sienta mejor?

That one... until I feel better?

Nurse

Casi. Hay que terminarlo todo, aunque se sienta mejor. Son diez días completos.

Almost. You have to finish all of it, even if you feel better. It’s a full ten days.

Patient

Ah, está bien.

Ah, all right.

Nurse

¿Por qué motivo llamaría al doctor?

What would make you call the doctor?

Patient

Si me da fiebre.

If I get a fever.

Nurse

Sí. ¿Y qué más?

Yes. And what else?

Patient

No me acuerdo.

I don’t remember.

Nurse

Si le falta el aire, o si el dolor empeora. Está escrito aquí, en español.

If you’re short of breath, or if the pain gets worse. It’s written here, in Spanish.

Nurse

Y si tiene dudas en casa, llame. No espere.

And if you have doubts at home, call. Don’t wait.

What made this work

Teach-back after an interpreted session is not redundant — it is where you find the one instruction that did not land. Correcting «until I feel better» is worth the whole ten minutes.

Dialogue 8 — The patient who says yes and means «I heard you»

You have just explained a bowel prep. The patient is nodding and smiling.

Nurse

¿Me entiende?

Do you understand me?

Patient

Sí, sí. Gracias, muy amable.

Yes, yes. Thank you, that’s very kind.

[ Nothing here is confirmation. Start over with a different kind of question. ]

Nurse

¿Qué parte quiere que le explique otra vez?

What part would you like me to explain again?

Patient

Pues... todo, la verdad.

Well... all of it, honestly.

Nurse

Gracias por decirme. No expliqué bien; vamos otra vez, más despacio.

Thank you for telling me. I didn’t explain it well; let’s go again, more slowly.

Nurse

Y voy a llamar al intérprete, porque esto tiene horarios y no quiero que se pierda nada.

And I’m going to call the interpreter, because this has a schedule and I don’t want anything to be lost.

What made this work

«¿Qué parte quiere que le explique otra vez?» assumes there is a part. That assumption is what gives a polite patient permission to say «all of it».

Write your own

Take the conversation you most often get stuck in and script it. Write the nurse's lines only — you cannot script the patient, and trying to is how nurses end up not listening.
