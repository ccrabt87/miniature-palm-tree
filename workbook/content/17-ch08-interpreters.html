Chapter 8

Working With Interpreters

The most important clinical Spanish skill is knowing when to stop using yours. This chapter covers the rules, the technique, and the documentation.

The legal floor (United States)

  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits national-origin discrimination by programs receiving federal financial assistance. Federal guidance treats denying meaningful language access to people with limited English proficiency (LEP) as a form of that discrimination.
  • Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act and its regulations (45 CFR Part 92, most recently revised in 2024) require covered health programs to take reasonable steps to give each LEP individual meaningful access, including qualified interpreters, free of charge.
  • Under those rules, a covered entity generally may not:
    • require patients to bring their own interpreter;
    • rely on a minor child to interpret, except in an emergency involving an imminent threat to safety when no qualified interpreter is immediately available;
    • rely on an accompanying adult, except in such an emergency, or when the patient specifically asks for it, the adult agrees, and it is appropriate in the circumstances;
    • rely on staff who are not qualified to interpret.
  • The Joint Commission and many state laws add their own requirements. Your facility’s language-access policy is the rulebook you follow on shift.
“Qualified” is a defined standardBeing bilingual is not the same as being a qualified interpreter. A qualified interpreter has shown proficiency in both languages, interprets accurately and impartially, knows the medical vocabulary, and follows interpreter ethics such as confidentiality. Bilingual staff who interpret usually must be assessed by the facility. Finishing this workbook does not qualify you.

When your Spanish is enough — and when it is not

Your Spanish is usually fine forCall a qualified interpreter for
  • Greetings, introductions, small talk, comfort
  • Routine care: vitals, repositioning, meals, hygiene
  • Simple yes/no checks (“Do you have pain?”)
  • Orienting to the room, call light, bed
  • The first seconds of an emergency, until help arrives
  • Telling the patient the interpreter is coming
  • Admission history, allergies, medication reconciliation
  • Informed consent for any procedure, blood or research
  • Diagnoses, test results, prognosis, bad news
  • Medication and discharge teaching
  • Pain or symptom answers you did not fully understand
  • Mental-health, abuse, sexual-health or safety screening
  • Goals of care, code status, end of life
  • Any time the patient or family asks for one

Running the encounter

Before (the 30-second pre-session)

  • Tell the interpreter who you are, the patient’s name, and the goal: “Discharge teaching for new insulin, about 10 minutes.”
  • Flag anything sensitive or any family dynamic they should know about.
  • For video or phone interpreting, position the screen or speaker so the patient can see and hear clearly, and check the volume first.

During

  • Speak to the patient, not to the interpreter. Look at the patient. Use first person: “How is your pain?”, not “Ask her how her pain is.”
  • Short segments. One or two sentences, then pause. Avoid idioms, abbreviations and jokes.
  • Expect everything said to be interpreted — including side comments to colleagues. Have those conversations outside the room.
  • Let the interpreter ask for clarification. If an exchange seems much longer or shorter than what you said, ask what was said.
  • Finish with teach-back through the interpreter.

After

  • Ask the interpreter if there was anything culturally or linguistically important you might have missed.

Documentation

Most facilities expect you to chart, for each interpreted encounter:

  • the patient’s preferred language, and whether an interpreter was offered and used or declined;
  • the interpreter’s name or ID number and modality (in-person, video, phone);
  • what was discussed and the result of teach-back;
  • if a patient insisted on a family member, that a qualified interpreter was offered free of charge and declined, and that one was still used for critical information if policy requires.
