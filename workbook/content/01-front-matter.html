Before You Begin

About This Workbook

This book is a bedside communication tool. It will make you a more present, more understandable nurse at the bedside of a Spanish-speaking patient. It will not make you an interpreter, and it is not meant to replace one.

Read this first — scope and safety

This workbook does not qualify you to interpret. In the United States, patients with limited English proficiency have a right to language assistance under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act. Consent, diagnosis, prognosis, treatment options, procedures, medication changes, code status, and discharge teaching all require a qualified medical interpreter — in person, by phone, or by video — and your facility's policy is the final word.

What the phrases in this book are good for: orienting a frightened person, greeting and reassuring, taking a first pass at a symptom, positioning and repositioning, explaining what you are about to do to someone's arm, keeping a patient with you during the ninety seconds it takes for the interpreter line to connect, and closing the small distances that make a hospital stay bearable. That is not a small job. Chapter 8 draws the line between the two in detail.

Nothing here is medical advice or a clinical protocol. Dosing, assessment findings, and red-flag thresholds mentioned in examples are illustrative only. Follow your own institution's orders, protocols, and scope of practice.

A note on the Spanish in this book

Spanish is not one thing. A Puerto Rican grandmother, a Guatemalan farmworker, a Venezuelan engineer, and a third-generation Mexican-American teenager share a language the way English speakers in Glasgow, Lagos, and Alabama share one. This book teaches a neutral Latin American Spanish that will be understood everywhere, and flags the places where regional words diverge enough to matter clinically — the word for “bus,” you can guess at; the word for “itching” you cannot.

Every phrase is given in the formal usted form. Chapter 6 explains why that default is the right one in a hospital, and the narrow circumstances in which you would move away from it.

How the phrases are written

Each phrase appears three ways: what you mean in English, what to say in Spanish, and how to say it. The third column is not the International Phonetic Alphabet. It is an English-reader's respelling, with the stressed syllable in capitals:
