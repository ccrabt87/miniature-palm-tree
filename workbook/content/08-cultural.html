Chapter Six

Cultural Competency Notes

Everything in this chapter is a pattern you may meet, not a rule about a person. “Latino patients believe X” is never true — the label covers twenty-odd countries, every social class, and four generations of immigration. Use these notes to know what to ask about, never to predict what someone thinks.

The only technique in this chapter that always works

Ask. ¿Hay algo que deba saber sobre usted o su familia para cuidarlo(a) mejor? — Is there anything I should know about you or your family so I can take better care of you? One sentence, asked without an agenda, outperforms every generalization printed below.

Names, and getting them right

Most Spanish-speaking patients carry two surnames: the father's first, then the mother's. María Elena Rodríguez Gutiérrez is Ms. Rodríguez, not Ms. Gutiérrez, and not Ms. Rodriguez-Gutierrez unless she writes it that way. Married women often keep both and may add de plus a husband's name socially, while their legal documents keep the birth surnames.

This matters beyond courtesy. Registration systems that jam both surnames into one field, or drop the second, create duplicate charts and lost results. When a patient's record cannot be found, search the second surname before you conclude they are new.

How do you spell your last name?¿Cómo se escribe su apellido?
Do you have two last names?¿Tiene dos apellidos?
Which one do you use here?¿Cuál usa aquí?
Have you been treated here under another name?¿Lo(a) han atendido aquí con otro nombre?
How would you like me to address you?¿Cómo prefiere que le llame?

Usted, tú, and titles

Spanish forces you to choose a level of formality in every sentence. In a hospital, the default is usted — for adults, always, including adults your own age. It is the register of respect, and using tú with an older patient reads roughly the way calling a seventy-year-old stranger “buddy” reads in English.

Don and doña before a first name — don Roberto, doña Elena — carry warmth and deference together. With an older patient who has been in the hospital several days, they can change the temperature of a room.

Family: who is in the room, and why

You may find six people in a room built for two, a spokesperson who answers questions addressed to the patient, and a family that expects to be consulted on decisions a US chart treats as individual. This is not interference. In much of Latin America, serious illness is managed by the family as a unit, and being left alone with bad news is the frightening outcome, not the private one.

How to work with it rather than around it

  • Address the patient, every time. Even when a relative answers, keep your body and your eyes on the patient. Then ask them directly: ¿Usted qué dice?
  • Ask who they want involved. ¿Con quién quiere que hablemos sobre su salud? Document the answer — it is both respectful and legally useful.
  • Ask how much they want to know. Some patients genuinely prefer that results go to a son or daughter first. That is a valid choice, and you can chart it: ¿Quiere que le demos los resultados a usted, o prefiere que hablemos primero con alguien de su familia?
  • Name a spokesperson out loud. With a large family, ask who will be the contact and give updates through that person. Everyone relaxes.

The family member who asks you not to tell the patient

“No le diga que es cáncer” comes from love and from a real tradition of protective non-disclosure. It also collides with the patient's legal right to their own information. Do not agree, and do not argue in the hallway. Say that you understand the wish to protect them, that you will not force information on anyone, and that you will ask the patient how much they want to know. Then escalate to the attending, the interpreter, and social work or ethics — this is not a bedside nurse's decision to make alone.

Why “sí” sometimes means “I heard you”

Two habits combine here. Respeto makes contradicting a clinician uncomfortable, especially for older patients and especially toward doctors. Simpatía puts a high value on pleasant, conflict-free interaction. The result is a patient who nods, says sí, thanks you warmly, and has not understood a word.

The fix is not to ask harder. It is to stop asking yes-or-no questions about understanding:

Instead ofAsk
¿Me entiende?¿Me puede decir con sus palabras cómo lo va a hacer en casa?
¿Tiene preguntas?¿Qué preguntas tiene?
¿Está claro?¿Qué parte quiere que le explique otra vez?
¿Va a tomar la medicina?¿Qué le podría hacer difícil tomarla?

Each right-hand question assumes there is something to say. That assumption is what gives a polite patient permission to disagree with you.

Personalismo: the ninety seconds that buy you the history

Warm, personal contact before business is not small talk to be skipped when you are busy — for many patients it is the thing that establishes you as trustworthy. Sitting down, at eye level, for one minute, will get you more history than five minutes of standing questions.

Good morning. How did you sleep?Buenos días. ¿Cómo durmió?
May I sit down for a minute?¿Me puedo sentar un momento?
Where are you from originally?¿De dónde es usted originalmente?
Is your family able to visit?¿Su familia puede venir a visitarlo(a)?
I'm sorry you're going through this.Siento mucho que esté pasando por esto.
I'm going to take good care of you.Lo(a) voy a cuidar bien.

How to ask, in one breath, without judgment

Muchas personas usan remedios en casa además de la medicina del doctor. ¿Usted usa algo? No hay ningún problema — sólo necesito saber para que nada le haga daño.

“Many people use remedies at home along with the doctor's medicine. Do you use anything? It's not a problem — I just need to know so nothing harms you.” The phrase muchas personas does the work: it makes the answer normal before the patient has to give it.

Faith at the bedside

Si Dios quiere (“God willing”) is often ordinary speech, not a refusal of treatment. Todo está en las manos de Dios can mean deep trust, or it can mean a patient has stopped believing the plan will work. You cannot tell which from the phrase — so ask.

Is your faith important to you here?¿Su fe es importante para usted aquí?
Would you like us to call the chaplain?¿Quiere que llamemos al capellán?
Would you like a priest / a pastor?¿Quiere un sacerdote / un pastor?
Is there anything your faith asks of us in your care?¿Hay algo que su fe pida en su cuidado?
Do you want a moment to pray before we start?¿Quiere un momento para rezar antes de empezar?

Modesty and gender

For many patients — not all, and not predictably — exposure in front of a clinician of another gender is genuinely distressing rather than merely awkward. It costs you nothing to offer.

Would you prefer a female nurse?¿Prefiere una enfermera mujer?
Would you prefer a male nurse?¿Prefiere un enfermero hombre?
Would you like someone to stay in the room?¿Quiere que alguien se quede en el cuarto?
I'll cover you. Only this part will be uncovered.Lo(a) voy a cubrir. Sólo esta parte va a quedar destapada.
Tell me if you feel uncomfortable at any moment.Dígame si se siente incómodo(a) en cualquier momento.

Fear that has nothing to do with the illness

Patients who are undocumented, who live in mixed-status households, or who are simply uninsured may delay care, minimize symptoms, give an incomplete address, decline follow-up, or leave before discharge is complete. None of that is a personality trait; it is a rational response to risk.

  • Do not ask about immigration status. It is not clinically necessary, and asking it will cost you the rest of the history.
  • Say the interpreter is free — gratis — and that it does not affect the bill.
  • Explain what you write down and who sees it, if a patient hesitates over an address or a phone number.
  • Bring in financial counseling early, not at discharge. Fear of a bill quietly rewrites a discharge plan.
