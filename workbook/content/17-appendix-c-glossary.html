Appendix C

Two-Way Glossary

Two lists, because they do two different jobs. The first is for producing Spanish — you know the English word and need to say it. The second is for understanding it — a patient has just said something and you need to know what it was. Nurses under-build the second list and then wonder why comprehension lags.

Gender, quickly

Nouns are listed with their article because the article is part of the word. El and los mark masculine; la and las mark feminine. Get it wrong and you will still be understood — this is a courtesy of accuracy, not a prerequisite for speaking.

Adjectives shift with the person: a man is alérgico, a woman is alérgica. Where this book writes alérgico(a), say only one of the two.

Part two — What patients say to you

Seventy sentences you will hear rather than say, grouped by what they mean rather than alphabetically — symptoms first, then questions, then the things patients say about money, language, medication, and family. Learning to recognise these is faster than learning to produce them, and it is the half of the skill that keeps you from nodding at something important.

Three of these deserve a stop

Me lo recetó un doctor en mi país and Es de hierbas both mean a medication list is incomplete — ask what, how much, and how often, and get the bottle if it exists. No tengo cómo llegar means the follow-up appointment you just made will not happen; that is a case-management referral before discharge, not a no-show two weeks from now.

The last page

You will not finish this book and be fluent. You will finish it able to walk into a room, greet someone in their own language, find out what hurts, tell them what you are about to do, and know exactly when to pick up the phone. That combination — real competence inside clear limits — is what a patient needs from a nurse at two in the morning. It is worth the eight weeks.
