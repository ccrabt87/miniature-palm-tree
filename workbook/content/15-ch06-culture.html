Chapter 6

Cultural Competency Notes

“Hispanic” and “Latino” describe tens of millions of people of every race, religion, income and politics, with roots in more than twenty countries. Use these notes as questions to ask, never as conclusions about the person in front of you.

Who you may be caring for

  • The 2020 U.S. Census counted 62.1 million Hispanic or Latino people, 18.7% of the population. Most are U.S.-born, and most speak English well. Many prefer Spanish for medical conversations even so, especially when frightened, in pain, or ill.
  • Roughly six in ten have Mexican heritage. Large communities also trace their roots to Puerto Rico, Central America (El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras), Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Colombia, Venezuela and elsewhere. Vocabulary differs between them; when a word is not landing, ask: ¿Cómo le dicen ustedes? (What do you call it?)
  • Spanish may not be the patient’s first language. Many people from Guatemala and southern Mexico speak an Indigenous language such as K’iche’, Mam, Q’anjob’al, Mixteco or Zapoteco. Ask ¿Qué idioma habla mejor? (Which language do you speak best?) and request an interpreter for that language.

Values that often shape the encounter

ConceptWhat it can look likeWhat helps
Respeto
respect		Patients may not question the nurse or doctor, may nod “yes” to be polite, and may expect formal address.Use usted, Señor / Señora, or Don / Doña + first name for elders. Use teach-back, not “Do you understand?”
Personalismo
personal warmth		Trust is built through the relationship more than through credentials.Introduce yourself, sit down, make eye contact, ask one personal question. A minute of small talk is not wasted time.
Confianza
trust, confidence		Once earned, patients may share more and follow through better.Keep promises (“I’ll be back in 10 minutes”). Consistency matters more than fluency.
Familismo
family first		Several relatives at the bedside; decisions made with the family; a spokesperson who is not the patient.Ask the patient who they want involved and what may be shared. The patient is still the decision-maker unless they say otherwise.
Simpatía
smooth, pleasant relations		Patients may avoid complaining, disagreeing, or reporting pain or side effects.Ask specifically and normalise: “Many people feel dizzy with this medicine — have you?”
Fe / religión
faith		Prayer, saints’ medals or rosaries at the bedside; a belief that outcomes are in God’s hands (si Dios quiere).Leave religious items in place when safe. Offer the chaplain. Faith and following the treatment plan usually go together.

Health beliefs and home remedies

Many families, of every background, use home remedies alongside medicine. Ask without judgement, because some interact with drugs.

Register, names and courtesy

  • Names. Many people use two surnames: father’s first, then mother’s (María López García). Register the full name exactly as the patient gives it to avoid record mix-ups, and ask which name to use: ¿Cómo prefiere que le llame?
  • Greetings matter. Greet everyone in the room. Leave with Con permiso (excuse me) and Que se mejore (get well).
  • Modesty. Explain before you touch: Le voy a revisar el abdomen. ¿Está bien? Offer a same-gender caregiver for intimate care when possible.
  • Bad news. Families may ask that a diagnosis not be told directly to an elder. Do not refuse or agree on the spot: ask the patient, through an interpreter, how much they want to know and who should be told. Involve the provider and, if needed, ethics.
  • Immigration fears. Fear of immigration consequences can stop people seeking care. Explain what the hospital does with information truthfully, without promising what you cannot guarantee, and refer to social work.
