This book does not make you an interpreter

Nothing in this workbook qualifies you to interpret, and nothing in it replaces a qualified medical interpreter. Federal civil-rights law (Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act) requires most U.S. health-care providers that receive federal funds to give patients with limited English proficiency meaningful access to care through qualified language assistance, free of charge. Chapter 8 explains what that means on shift.

Use the Spanish in this book to greet, comfort, orient, do routine bedside care, and recognise an emergency. Use a qualified interpreter for consent, diagnoses, teaching, discharge, medication histories that matter, bad news, and anything you are not sure you understood.

Who this is for

Registered nurses, LPNs/LVNs, nursing students, CNAs and patient-care techs in U.S. hospitals, clinics, long-term care and home health who care for Spanish-speaking patients and want to communicate more safely and more humanely. No prior Spanish is assumed; if you already speak some, the clinical chapters and quizzes will sharpen it.

The Spanish in this book

  • Neutral Latin-American Spanish. Spanish is spoken in more than twenty countries. Where a common regional word differs, both are given (e.g. la cobija / la manta, blanket).
  • Usted throughout. With adult patients, use the formal “you” (usted) until invited otherwise. Chapter 9 shows when tú is right (young children).
  • Gender. Adjectives that describe the patient change ending: ¿Está cansado? to a man, ¿Está cansada? to a woman. Where it matters we write cansado/a.
  • Pronunciation guides appear in small grey text, with the stressed syllable in CAPITALS: dolor doh-LOHR.

Disclaimer

This workbook is an educational communication aid. It is not medical advice, not a clinical protocol, and not legal advice. Follow your facility’s policies, order sets and language-access procedures. Discharge templates in Chapter 7 are starting points that must be reviewed and adapted by your organisation, and filled in from the provider’s actual orders, before use with patients. Medication names, doses and return precautions must always come from the patient’s chart.
