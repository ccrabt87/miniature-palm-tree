Chapter Three

The Admission Interview

Sixty questions, in the order you actually ask them. Work down the page and you will have a history. Stop at the red boxes and get an interpreter — those are the questions where a near-miss answer becomes a chart error.

Before question 1

If the patient's answers will drive consent, a diagnosis, a treatment decision, or code status, start the encounter with an interpreter rather than switching to one halfway through. Offer the interpreter first, in Spanish, and document the offer:

Tenemos intérpretes gratis. ¿Quiere que llame a un intérprete?

teh-NEH-mos een-TER-preh-tehs GRAH-tees. KYEH-reh keh YAH-meh ah oon een-TER-preh-teh

The word gratis matters. Patients decline interpreters they believe they will be billed for.

Question 7 is not a formality

Ask it before you ask about drug use, pregnancy, violence at home, or immigration- adjacent topics. The answer is sometimes no, and the only way to find that out is to ask while the family member still assumes it is routine.

“It makes my stomach hurt” is not an allergy

Question 18 is the whole point of this block. Patients report intolerances as allergies and true anaphylaxis as “a little reaction.” Always ask what happened, and chart the reaction, not just the word allergy. If the answer is complicated or the patient is unsure, get an interpreter — this is a field where a misheard answer changes what gets hung.

Ask 27 out loud, every time, without a face

Herbal and imported remedies are common, clinically real, and routinely omitted because patients expect to be judged. Manzanilla (chamomile) is harmless; imported antibiotics bought without a prescription, weight-loss teas, and some traditional preparations are not. Ask neutrally, the same way you asked about vitamins, and thank the patient for telling you.

Words you will hear: té de manzanilla, sábila (aloe), hierbabuena (mint), uña de gato, nopal, ajo (garlic), inyecciones brought from abroad.

Question 47 earns its place

“When was your last drink?” is the question that gets withdrawal onto the care plan before day two. Ask it of everyone who answers yes to 46, without adjusting your tone.

Questions 59 and 60 are asked alone

Never screen for intimate partner violence through a family member, a friend, or a bilingual visitor, and never through a child. Clear the room first — Necesito hablar con el paciente a solas unos minutos — and use a professional interpreter, by phone if that is what is available. Follow your facility's protocol for a positive screen; do not improvise.

Do not ask about immigration status. It is not clinically necessary, and asking it costs you every answer that comes after.

Never ask “¿Me entiende?” and stop there

“Do you understand me?” invites a yes from anyone who is tired, polite, or embarrassed — which is nearly everyone in a hospital bed. Ask the patient to say it back instead: Para estar seguro(a) de que expliqué bien, ¿me puede decir con sus palabras lo que va a hacer? That sentence puts the burden on your explanation rather than on their comprehension, and it is the only kind of check that catches a misunderstanding.
