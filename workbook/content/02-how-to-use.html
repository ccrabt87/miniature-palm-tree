How to Use This Workbook

There is a version of this book that lives in your bag and gets read on breaks, and a version that lives on the unit and gets grabbed at 3 a.m. Set it up for both.

Three ways in

1. The eight-week pass

Work one chapter a week in order. The chapters are built to stack: pronunciation makes the vocabulary usable, the vocabulary makes the interview possible, the interview is what the medication and discharge scripts assume. Each week, take the chapter quiz in Chapter 10 before you look at Chapter 11. Wrong answers are the only part of a quiz worth anything.

2. The night-shift grab

Print the Pocket Reference Card in Chapter 12, cut it out, laminate it, clip it to your badge reel. Tab Chapter 5 — the emergency flashcards — because that is the chapter you will need when there is no time to look anything up. Everything else can wait for a slower night.

3. The targeted fix

You already speak some Spanish but freeze in one specific place: explaining a medication, or taking a history, or telling a family what is happening in a code. Go straight to that chapter, drill its tables out loud, and use its fillable practice pages.

The rule that makes all of it work: say it out loud

Reading Spanish silently builds recognition. Recognition is not retrieval. At the bedside you need retrieval — the phrase has to arrive in your mouth while you are also watching a monitor. The only way to build that is to speak the phrase aloud, at full volume, many more times than feels necessary. Ten spoken repetitions beat an hour of reading.

A drill that takes four minutes

  1. Cover the Spanish column with your hand. Read the English line.
  2. Say the Spanish out loud before you uncover it. Guess if you have to.
  3. Uncover. If you were wrong, say the correct phrase aloud three times.
  4. Move down the table. At the bottom, go back to any line you missed.

Do this with one table per shift. In eight weeks you will have done the whole book.

Filling it in on screen or on paper

Every blank line in this book is a real form field. Open the PDF in any standard reader — Acrobat, Preview, Edge, most tablet annotation apps — and you can type your unit's phone number, your patients' most common home medications, and your own discharge wording directly into the page and save it. Or print it and use a pen. The discharge templates in Chapter 7 are designed to be filled in once, per unit, and then photocopied.

A last thing before Chapter 1

You are going to mispronounce words in front of patients. You will conjugate something backwards and a family member will smile. This is fine — better than fine. The patient in front of you has almost certainly spent the day being spoken about rather than spoken to. An imperfect sentence aimed directly at them lands differently than a perfect one aimed at their daughter. Accuracy matters most exactly where this book tells you to stop and call an interpreter; everywhere else, effort counts more than grammar.
