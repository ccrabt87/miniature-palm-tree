Chapter Nine

Pediatric & Geriatric Adaptations

At both ends of life the Spanish changes — the grammar of formality, the size of the words, the person you are actually talking to. Most of what goes wrong at these two extremes is a register problem, not a vocabulary problem.

Part one: children

Switch to tú, and shrink the words

With a child under about twelve, usted sounds cold and strange. Use tú. Spanish also has a built-in kindness setting: the diminutive. Adding -ito or -ita makes a thing small, gentle, and less frightening. Un piquete is a poke; un piquetito is nothing much at all.

Procedures, without lying about them

Never say no te va a doler about something that will. You get one chance at a child's trust, and the next nurse pays for spending it.

It's going to hurt a little, and it will be quick.Te va a doler un poquito, y va a ser rápido.
You can hold my hand. Or your mom's.Puedes agarrar mi mano. O la de tu mamá.
Let's count together: one, two, three.Vamos a contar juntos: uno, dos, tres.
Blow hard, like blowing out candles.Sopla fuerte, como si apagaras unas velitas.

The rule that gets broken most often

The bilingual ten-year-old in the room is not your interpreter, and neither is the teenager who has done it for the family since grade school — not for a symptom, not for a consent, not once at three in the morning. If a child is the only bilingual person present, that is precisely when you call the phone line. Say it out loud so the family understands it is a rule and not a judgment: No podemos pedirle a un niño que traduzca. Voy a llamar a un intérprete — es gratis.

Adolescents

Start with usted and let them move you off it. Get one-on-one time the same way you would in English, and normalize it to the parent first.

I talk with all my teenage patients alone for a few minutes.Con todos mis pacientes adolescentes hablo unos minutos a solas.
Would you mind stepping out for a moment?¿Le importaría salir un momento?
What we talk about is private, with a few exceptions I'll explain.Lo que hablamos es privado, con algunas excepciones que te explico.
Do you have any questions you'd rather ask without your family here?¿Tienes alguna pregunta que prefieras hacer sin tu familia?

Part two: older adults

Being heard

Shouting raises pitch, and age-related hearing loss takes the high frequencies first — so shouting makes you harder to understand, in any language. Drop your pitch, slow down, face the patient, and let them see your mouth.

Can you hear me well?¿Me escucha bien?
Do you want me to speak more slowly?¿Quiere que hable más despacio?
Do you have your hearing aids with you?¿Trae sus aparatos para oír?
Which ear do you hear better with?¿De qué oído escucha mejor?
Do you need your glasses?¿Necesita sus lentes?
Would you like this written down bigger?¿Quiere que se lo escriba más grande?

Confusion in a second language is not always confusion

Older adults who learned English late often lose it first — under stress, in delirium, after anesthesia, or with dementia. A patient who answered in English on Monday and only in Spanish on Wednesday may be delirious, or may simply have fallen back to a first language. Screen for delirium properly rather than reading the language shift as the finding, and get an interpreter for any cognitive assessment: a mental status exam done in a language the patient half-speaks measures nothing.

Ask these alone, with an interpreter

Elder mistreatment screening, like intimate partner violence screening, is done without family in the room and never through a relative:

¿Se siente seguro(a) en su casa? · ¿Alguien le ha gritado, lastimado o amenazado? · ¿Alguien maneja su dinero? · ¿Alguien le ha quitado su dinero o sus medicinas? · ¿Hay veces que lo(a) dejan solo(a) cuando necesita ayuda?

Follow your facility's protocol and mandatory reporting requirements for any positive answer.

Goals of care

These conversations belong to an interpreter, every time — but knowing what they sound like helps you recognize when one has started by accident, and stop it until the interpreter is on the line.

Have you talked with your family about what you would want if you got very sick?¿Ha hablado con su familia sobre qué querría si se pusiera muy enfermo(a)?
Who would speak for you if you couldn't?¿Quién hablaría por usted si usted no pudiera?
Do you have any documents about your wishes?¿Tiene algún documento sobre sus deseos?
This is important. Let me get an interpreter so we do it right.Esto es importante. Déjeme llamar a un intérprete para hacerlo bien.
