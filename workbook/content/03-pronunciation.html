Chapter One

Sounds First

Spanish pronunciation is close to perfectly regular. Learn eleven rules and you can read aloud, accurately, a word you have never seen — including a patient's surname and the name of a drug. That skill is worth more on a unit than two hundred memorized phrases.

The five vowels

English has somewhere between eleven and twenty vowel sounds depending on who is counting. Spanish has five, they never change, and they are short and clean. The single most common thing that makes an English speaker hard to understand in Spanish is gliding a vowel — letting o drift toward ow, or e toward ey. Cut it off short.

Drill

Say these five, clipping every vowel short: sangre · pecho · herida · dolor · orina. Then say them again while holding a hand flat under your chin. If your jaw is travelling a long way on the o of dolor, you are gliding.

The trill is optional. The vowels are not.

Nurses lose weeks trying to roll an r. Skip it. A single flap in place of a trill marks you as a foreigner; it does not make you unintelligible. A glided vowel or a misplaced stress can genuinely change a word. Spend the practice time on stress.

Stress: three rules and an accent mark

Every Spanish word has one stressed syllable, and you can find it without ever having heard the word:

  1. If the word carries a written accent, stress that syllable. Done. corazón is koh-rah-SOHN.
  2. No accent, and the word ends in a vowel, n, or s → stress the second-to-last syllable. enfermera en-fer-MEH-rah.
  3. No accent, ending in any other consonant → stress the last syllable. doctor dok-TOR; hospital os-pee-TAHL.

This matters clinically because stress carries grammar. Compare:

The question that sounds like a question

Spanish does not reorder words to ask something. ¿Tiene dolor? and Tiene dolor. are the same three words; the pitch rise at the end is the only thing making one a question. English speakers under-do this rise, and patients answer a question they did not realize was being asked. Overshoot it deliberately — go up higher than feels natural on the final syllable.

Practice: read these aloud with a hard rise at the end

¿Tiene dolor? · ¿Puede respirar? · ¿Es alérgico a algún medicamento? · ¿Me entiende?

Cognates you can simply borrow

A large share of clinical vocabulary is Latin on both sides. Learn the endings and you get hundreds of words free.

Don't invent cognates during a clinical conversation

The pattern works often enough to be dangerous. Embarrassed is not embarazada; severe is grave, not severo (which means strict); to realize is darse cuenta, not realizar (to carry out). If a word matters, look it up or ask the interpreter.
