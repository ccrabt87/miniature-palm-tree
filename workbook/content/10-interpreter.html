Chapter Eight

Working With Interpreters

The most useful Spanish skill a nurse can have is knowing exactly when to stop using it. This chapter draws that line, and then makes you good at the thing on the other side of it — running an interpreted encounter well, which is a clinical skill in its own right.

Why this is not optional

Under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, US healthcare organizations that receive federal funds must provide meaningful language access to patients with limited English proficiency, at no cost to the patient. Accreditation standards point the same way. In practice that means qualified interpreters — people who have been assessed for language proficiency and trained in medical interpreting and ethics — not whoever is available and bilingual.

Your facility's language-access policy is the operative document, and it will be more specific than this page. Find it, read it once, and know how to reach the interpreter line from a patient room without looking anything up.

Always call an interpreter

These conversations, every time, without exception

  • Informed consent for any procedure, surgery, anesthesia, blood product, or research
  • Giving a diagnosis, a prognosis, or results of any significance
  • Explaining treatment options, risks, or alternatives
  • Code status, advance directives, goals of care, hospice, withdrawal of treatment
  • Death notification, and conversations with a grieving family
  • Admission history and physical, and full discharge teaching
  • Any new medication, any dose change, and all medication reconciliation
  • Psychiatric assessment, suicide risk screening, capacity evaluation
  • Intimate partner violence, abuse, or neglect screening
  • Anything the patient asks for an interpreter for — the request settles it
  • Any moment you are not certain you were understood

Where your own Spanish earns its keep

Everything else, roughly: greeting and orienting, comfort and positioning, telling someone what you are about to do to their arm, hourly rounding, pain checks, holding a frightened patient's attention while the interpreter line connects, and the hundred small exchanges that make a person feel like a person. That is a large and genuinely valuable territory. It is not the territory above.

A test that works at the bedside

Ask yourself: if the patient's answer came back garbled, would the chart or the plan change? If yes, get the interpreter. If the worst case is that someone gets the wrong flavor of gelatin, go ahead in Spanish.

Family members are not interpreters

The daughter who has interpreted for her mother since she was nine is competent, loyal, and completely wrong for this job. The reasons are not about her ability:

  • Accuracy. Untrained interpreters summarize, soften, and skip. Studies of ad hoc interpreting consistently find more errors of clinical consequence than with trained interpreters.
  • Editing to protect. Families omit frightening words, and patients omit symptoms they do not want their family to hear.
  • Confidentiality. Sexual history, substance use, pregnancy, violence at home, mental health — none of it gets said accurately through a relative.
  • Coercion. If the person interpreting is the person a patient is afraid of, you have built the abuse into the encounter.
  • The burden. Relatives have to carry the news they just delivered.

Children are never interpreters. Not for a symptom, not for a consent, not for “just this once at three in the morning.” Ask an adult to step out with the child and use the phone line.

That last line does most of the work. It keeps the relative in the room and takes the job off them.

Running the encounter: twelve rules

  1. Brief the interpreter first. Thirty seconds outside the room: who the patient is, what the conversation is for, and anything sensitive coming.
  2. Introduce the interpreter to the patient and say the interpreter is bound by confidentiality.
  3. Position yourself facing the patient, interpreter beside or slightly behind you. Your body language belongs to the patient.
  4. Speak to the patient, not the interpreter. Say “How long have you had the pain?” — never “Ask her how long...”
  5. Look at the patient while the interpreter speaks. That is when you learn the most: the face during the Spanish tells you what the words will not.
  6. One or two sentences at a time, then pause. Long paragraphs get summarized.
  7. Plain language. Drop the jargon, the acronyms, the idioms. “We need to rule out a bleed” is three problems in one sentence.
  8. Let the interpreter interpret everything, including what the family says in the corner and what you say to the tech at the door.
  9. Do not ask the interpreter for their opinion on the patient's culture, credibility, or plan. Do ask them to flag a word that has no equivalent.
  10. Expect the interpreter to interrupt. A good one will stop you to clarify or to note a cultural point. That is the job working correctly.
  11. Teach back through the interpreter, the same as you would in English.
  12. Allow more time. An interpreted encounter takes roughly twice as long. Plan for it rather than rushing at the end, which is where errors live.

When the patient declines an interpreter

Patients decline for reasons that are usually fixable: they think they will be billed, they do not want a stranger hearing their business, they are embarrassed about their English, or a relative has already appointed themselves. Work the reason.

It doesn't cost you anything, and it isn't on your bill.No le cuesta nada, y no aparece en su cuenta.
The interpreter can be on the phone, not in the room.El intérprete puede estar por teléfono, no en el cuarto.
Your English is good — this is for the difficult words.Su inglés es bueno — esto es para las palabras difíciles.
I'd feel better knowing I explained it right.Yo me sentiría más tranquilo(a) sabiendo que le expliqué bien.
We can try it, and stop if you don't like it.Podemos probar, y paramos si no le gusta.

If the patient still declines, document the offer, the decline, and how you proceeded. For consent and other high-stakes conversations, escalate rather than proceeding on a declined interpreter — that is a decision for the provider obtaining consent, and your facility will have a process.

What to document

  • The patient's preferred language, from the patient
  • That an interpreter was offered, and accepted or declined
  • Mode used: in person, video, or phone
  • Interpreter name or ID number, and agency if external
  • That teach-back was done and what the patient said back
  • If a family member was present, that they were present as family — not as the interpreter
